\begin{abstract}
Model-based controllers for agile quadrotors are highly sensitive
to three quantities that are seldom measured online: the total
mass $m$, the inner-loop angular-rate time constant $\tau_{rc}$,
and the thrust-actuator time constant $\tau_f$.  In practice these
values are copied from datasheets or hand-tuned, and as a result
the controller's dynamic model can disagree with the physical
platform by a factor of two on $\tau_{rc}$ and by $5\!-\!20\%$ on
$m$.  This paper proposes an \emph{observability-aware multi-rate
estimator} that recovers $(m,\tau_{rc},\tau_f)$ online from
in-flight signals while respecting the time scale and
identifiability of each parameter.

Four contributions:
\emph{(i)}~a Gramian-based identifiability analysis with a proof
of the hover singularity for the $(m,\vect d)$ pair, validated
empirically across three flight regimes;
\emph{(ii)}~a reduced-state $10$-D translational MHE that treats
the measured attitude as a known input, lifting QP success from
$<\!1\%$ to $100\%$;
\emph{(iii)}~two decoupled RLS estimators at $100$\,Hz that
identify $\tau_{rc}$ and $\tau_f$ on their own linear residuals;
\emph{(iv)}~a three-regime study (hover, slow, agile) that
quantifies the nominal-vs-real gap ($\tau_{rc}^{nom}\!=\!0.030$\,s
versus $\hat\tau_{rc}\!\approx\!0.056$\,s, mass bias
$\approx\!5\%$, $\hat\tau_f\!\approx\!8.4$\,ms) and demarcates
the regime where the identification matters (commissioning,
digital-twin calibration, INDI) from the one where the
fast-feedback NMPC absorbs the bias.

The full pipeline runs in real time inside the MuJoCo
software-in-the-loop simulator with a per-step parameter-estimation
cost below $4$\,ms and no QP failure across $63$\,s of agile flight.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Quadrotor, system identification, moving-horizon estimation,
observability, model predictive control, recursive least squares,
acados, multi-rate estimation.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{gile} multirotors are now routinely flown at the
limit of their actuators.  Model predictive controllers
(MPC)~\cite{hanover2024autonomous,romero2022model,foehn2021time}
and incremental nonlinear dynamic inversion
(INDI)~\cite{smeur2016adaptive,sun2022comparative} reach
double-digit speeds in racing, dynamic tracking, and aggressive
payload-delivery benchmarks.  All these controllers, however, are
only as good as the dynamic model that populates their internal
predictor: mass, body inertia, rate-loop time constant, thrust
time constant, drag coefficients and control effectiveness.  The
values are routinely taken from the manufacturer's datasheet,
copied across platforms, hand-tuned in flight, or simply left at
the nominal values shipped with a simulator.  The cost of this
practice is rarely audited.

In this work we audit it.  Using a high-fidelity MuJoCo quadrotor
as a ground-truth surrogate, we show that the nominal rate-loop
time constant $\tau_{rc}^{nom}=0.030$\,s assumed by the controller
under-predicts the actual $\hat\tau_{rc}\approx 0.056$\,s by
nearly a factor of two, that the nominal mass under-predicts the
real value by $\approx 5\%$, and that the thrust actuator
behaves with a time constant $\hat\tau_f\approx 9$\,ms that is
fully invisible to the controller at the standard $100$\,Hz rate.
In a classical estimation paradigm this combination of biases
would be addressed by augmenting the controller's state with
$(m,\tau_{rc},\tau_f,\vect d)$ and running a single moving-horizon
estimator (MHE) over the entire augmented vector.  In practice
such monolithic full-state MHEs are fragile: they require a
singular $\SOg$ log-map Jacobian in the arrival cost, integrate
the quaternion over windows of $\sim$\,1\,s, and---as we document
quantitatively---fail to solve the underlying QP on a majority of
real-time steps even on noiseless simulated data.

This paper takes a different stance.  We argue that the three
parameters $(m,\tau_{rc},\tau_f)$ live on three distinct time
scales and have three different observability structures, and
that trying to estimate them with a single estimator is the
source of the numerical fragility documented above.  Specifically:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Mass and translational disturbance} share the
translational equation $\dot{\vect v} = -g\vect e_3 + (f/m)\vect a + \vect d$.
They are jointly identifiable only when the regressor
$[f\vect a\,|\,\mat I_3]$ spans the relevant directions in
$\R^3$, which requires non-trivial attitude/thrust excitation over
a window long enough to average out measurement noise.
\item \emph{The rate-loop time constant $\tau_{rc}$} appears
linearly in the rotational equation
$\dot{\vect\omega}=k_\tau(\vect\omega_{cmd}-\vect\omega)$ with
$k_\tau=1/\tau_{rc}$.  Its observability is local in time and
driven by the rate commands.  No long horizon is needed.
\item \emph{The thrust time constant $\tau_f$} appears in the
actuator equation $\dot f = k_f(T-f)$ with $k_f=1/\tau_f$.  Its
observability requires reconstructing $f$ from the translational
acceleration once $m$ is known, but its time scale is typically
faster than the control rate.
\end{itemize}

Estimating each parameter at the rate compatible with its own
time scale, and on its own (observable) signal, sidesteps the
monolithic fragility and yields a numerically robust
identification pipeline.  The resulting architecture---a $10$-D
translational MHE running at $33$\,Hz over a $1$\,s window, plus
two RLS estimators running at $100$\,Hz on the rotational and
thrust residuals---is the central contribution of this paper.

\subsection{Notation on the two thrusts}
Throughout the paper we distinguish carefully between two
thrust-related quantities.  The symbol $T$ denotes the
\emph{commanded} collective thrust sent by the controller at
$100$\,Hz; it is the actual control input from the perspective of
the outer-loop NMPC.  The symbol $f$ denotes the \emph{actual}
collective thrust delivered to the airframe by the propellers;
it lags $T$ through the first-order actuator equation
$\dot f = (T-f)/\tau_f$.  Critically, the translational dynamics
in \eqref{eq:dv}~below contain $f$, not $T$.  At $100$\,Hz with
$\tau_f\approx 9$\,ms the two are nearly equal and the controller
may safely use $T\!\approx\!f$, but in the analysis we must keep
them distinct.

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph*{MHE for quadrotors}
The use of moving-horizon estimation for quadrotor mass and
disturbance has been explored
in~\cite{torrente2021data,nguyen2020model,liu2023nonlinear,kabzan2019learning}.
The state vector is typically augmented with mass, drag
coefficients and a translational disturbance, while the attitude
quaternion and angular velocity remain part of the estimated
state.  The most relevant
works~\cite{liu2023nonlinear,kabzan2019learning} report converged
mass estimates on simulated data but only briefly discuss the
conditions under which the parameters are recoverable.  A common
reported failure mode is loss of QP feasibility under window
lengths longer than $0.5$\,s, attributed to numerical
conditioning.  Our analysis shows that the same failure mode can
be eliminated structurally by removing the attitude state from
the estimator.

\paragraph*{Identifiability of quadrotor parameters}
Identifiability of multirotor parameters has been studied in the
classical~\cite{burri2018system} and online~\cite{wuest2019online}
settings.  These works define persistency-of-excitation conditions
for parameter identifiability and report empirical conditioning of
the data matrix.  Our contribution to this line is a closed-form
expression for the Gramian of the translational regressor and an
explicit characterisation of the hover singularity, together with
a direct empirical validation on agile flight.

\paragraph*{Inner-loop time-constant identification}
The identification of the body-rate controller's time constant
$\tau_{rc}$ has received less attention in the MPC literature
than mass and drag, even though MPC-based racing
controllers~\cite{romero2022model,foehn2021time} are highly
sensitive to its value through the inertia-equivalent body-rate
response.  In INDI~\cite{smeur2016adaptive,sun2022comparative},
by contrast, correct $\tau_{rc}$ identification is the bottleneck
of stability, since the controller inverts the rotational
dynamics directly.  Our RLS-based identification of $\tau_{rc}$
thus also addresses an open problem for INDI-style controllers.

\paragraph*{Thrust dynamics}
Thrust actuator dynamics are commonly modelled as a first-order
lag~\cite{bauersfeld2021neurobem} with a time constant in the
$5\!-\!20$\,ms range.  The standard practice is either to ignore
this lag or to apply a fixed feedforward compensation.  We
identify $\tau_f$ online with the second RLS and characterise
the regime in which the feedforward becomes useful (effectively
only when the controller's bandwidth approaches the actuator's).

\subsection{Identified gap and contributions}
The literature lacks an explicit observability analysis that
\emph{predicts} when joint mass/disturbance recovery succeeds,
and monolithic full-state MHEs are numerically fragile because
they couple parameters of incompatible time scales in a single
QP.  This paper makes four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A closed-form Gramian-based identifiability analysis with
a proof of the hover singularity (Section~\ref{sec:obs}).
\item A reduced-state $10$-D translational MHE that treats the
measured attitude as a known input, lifting QP success from
$<\!1\%$ to $100\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:mhe-t}).
\item Two decoupled RLS estimators at $100$\,Hz for $\tau_{rc}$
and $\tau_f$ on their own linear residuals (Section~\ref{sec:rls}).
\item A three-regime empirical study (hover, slow, agile) that
validates the analysis and quantifies the gap between nominal
and identified parameters under a $100$\,Hz NMPC
(Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:model} introduces the system model and notation.
Section~\ref{sec:obs} develops the identifiability analysis.
Sections~\ref{sec:mhe-t} and~\ref{sec:rls} present the MHE and the
two RLS estimators.  Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the
simulator setup and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
experimental results.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the
findings and their generalisation to hardware and to INDI.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{System Model and Notation}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Notation}
We use the following symbols throughout:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\vect p,\vect v\in\R^3$: position and linear velocity in
the world frame;
\item $\vect q\in\R^4$ with $\vect q^\top\vect q=1$: the
body-to-world unit quaternion, with rotation matrix
$\vect R(\vect q)\in\SOg$;
\item $\vect\omega\in\R^3$: body-frame angular velocity;
\item $T\in\R_{\ge0}$: \emph{commanded} collective thrust [N],
sent by the controller at $100$\,Hz;
\item $f\in\R_{\ge0}$: \emph{actual} collective thrust delivered
by the propellers [N], with $\dot f = (T-f)/\tau_f$;
\item $\vect\omega_{cmd}\in\R^3$: the body-rate command sent by
the controller to the inner-loop rate tracker;
\item $\vect e_3 = [0,0,1]^\top$, $g=9.81$\,m/s$^2$;
\item $\vect a(t)=\vect R(\vect q(t))\,\vect e_3\in\R^3$: the
unit-norm thrust direction in the world frame.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:notation} summarises the model parameters; the
\emph{Estimated by} column anticipates the architecture of
Sections~\ref{sec:mhe-t}--\ref{sec:rls}.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Model parameters and their physical meaning.}
\label{tab:notation}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning & Estimated by \\
\midrule
$m$                  & vehicle mass [kg]                        & Translational MHE \\
$\vect d$            & translational disturbance acc.\ [m/s$^2$]& Translational MHE \\

\subsection{Continuous-time dynamics}
The continuous-time quadrotor model used throughout the paper is
\begin{align}
\dot{\vect p} &= \vect v, \label{eq:dp}\\
\dot{\vect v} &= -g\,\vect e_3 + \frac{f}{m}\,\vect a(\vect q) + \vect d, \label{eq:dv}\\
\dot{\vect q} &= \tfrac{1}{2}\,\vect q \otimes [\,0,\;\vect\omega\,], \label{eq:dq}\\
\dot{\vect\omega} &= k_\tau(\vect\omega_{cmd}-\vect\omega), \label{eq:dw}\\
\dot{f} &= k_f\,(T - f), \label{eq:df}
\end{align}
where $\otimes$ denotes the Hamilton quaternion product.  The
control inputs to the physical platform are $(T,\vect\omega_{cmd})$.

\paragraph*{Two thrusts, two roles}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dv} contains the \emph{actual} thrust $f$,
whereas \eqref{eq:df} couples $f$ to the \emph{commanded} thrust
$T$ through the first-order lag with constant $\tau_f$.  The
controller sets $T$ at $100$\,Hz; the platform delivers $f$ with
bandwidth $1/\tau_f$.  For $\tau_f\ll \dt$, $f\approx T$ and the
lag is invisible to the controller.  We will see in
Section~\ref{sec:results} that this is precisely the regime of
the present MuJoCo platform.

\paragraph*{Rate loop as a first-order lag}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dw} models the inner-loop rate tracker as a
first-order system from $\vect\omega_{cmd}$ to $\vect\omega$,
with constant $\tau_{rc}=1/k_\tau$.  This is a standard reduction
of a high-bandwidth attitude controller and is consistent with
the formulation used by the MPC racing
literature~\cite{romero2022model,foehn2021time}.

\subsection{Parameter vector}
The parameter vector estimated in this paper is
\[
\vect\theta = (m,\,\tau_{rc},\,\tau_f)\in\R^3.
\]
The translational disturbance $\vect d\in\R^3$ is included as an
additional MHE state when offset-free behaviour is required.  We
will see that, on the platform used, $\vect d$ creates an
ambiguity with $m$ that biases mass estimation; we will therefore
also study a \emph{mass-only} configuration in which $\vect d$
is fixed at zero.

\subsection{Discrete-time form and measurements}
For implementation, \eqref{eq:dp}--\eqref{eq:df} are discretised
with an explicit fourth-order Runge--Kutta integrator at step
$\dt=0.01$\,s.  We assume that an on-board state estimator
(e.g., visual--inertial or motion-capture) provides
$(\vect p,\vect v,\vect q,\vect\omega)$ at $100$\,Hz with
Gaussian noise.  The controller publishes $(T,\vect\omega_{cmd})$
at the same rate.  All these signals are available to the
parameter estimator.

\section{Identifiability of the Translational Parameters}\label{sec:obs}

We study the identifiability of $(m,\vect d)$ from the
translational equation~\eqref{eq:dv} when the thrust $f(t)$ and
the thrust direction $\vect a(t)$ are treated as known inputs and
the linear velocity $\vect v(t)$ is measured.  The rotational and
thrust parameters $(\tau_{rc},\tau_f)$ are handled separately and
are structurally decoupled (Lemma~\ref{lem:decoup}).

\subsection{Linear regressor}
Defining the inverse-mass--disturbance parameter vector
\begin{equation}
\vect\theta_T \;=\; [\,1/m,\;d_x,\;d_y,\;d_z\,]^\top \in \R^4,
\end{equation}
equation~\eqref{eq:dv} can be rearranged into the linear
regressor form
\begin{equation}
\dot{\vect v}(t) + g\,\vect e_3 \;=\; \Phi(t)\,\vect\theta_T,
\quad
\Phi(t) \;=\;\big[\,f(t)\,\vect a(t)\;\big|\;\mat I_3\,\big] \in \R^{3\times 4}.
\label{eq:regressor}
\end{equation}
The first column of $\Phi(t)$ contains the time-varying input
$f(t)\vect a(t)\in\R^3$, while the last three columns form the
identity.  We work over a sliding window $[t-T,t]$ of length $T$
and define the cumulative \emph{identification Gramian}
\begin{equation}
\mat G(t,T) \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\int_{t-T}^{t}\!\!\Phi(\tau)^\top\Phi(\tau)\,d\tau \;\in\;\R^{4\times 4}.
\label{eq:gramian}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Identifiability]\label{lem:idf}
If $\mat G(t,T)\succ 0$ (i.e., positive definite) and the
measurement noise has bounded variance, then the unweighted
least-squares estimate
\[
\hat{\vect\theta}_T = \mat G(t,T)^{-1}\,\frac{1}{T}\int_{t-T}^{t}\!\!\Phi(\tau)^\top[\dot{\vect v}(\tau)+g\vect e_3]\,d\tau
\]
is consistent with covariance proportional to $\mat G^{-1}$.  If
$\mat G$ is rank deficient, then $\vect\theta_T$ is recoverable
only up to its null space.
\end{lemma}
\noindent The proof is the standard least-squares argument and is
omitted.

\subsection{Hover singularity}
\begin{proposition}[Hover singularity]\label{prop:hover}
At level hover, $\vect a(t)\equiv\vect e_3$ and
$f(t)\equiv mg$.  Then for any $T>0$,
\[
\mathrm{rank}\,\mat G(t,T) \;\le\; 3,
\]
with the one-dimensional null space spanned by
$[\,mg,\,0,\,0,\,-1\,]^\top$.  Equivalently, $1/m$ and $d_z$ are
not separable at hover; any estimator can recover only the linear
combination $g/m - d_z$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At level hover the first column of $\Phi(t)$ is
$f(t)\vect a(t)=mg\,\vect e_3$, which is collinear with the
fourth column $\vect e_3$ of $\mat I_3$.  Therefore
$\Phi(t)\,[mg,0,0,-1]^\top \equiv \vect 0$, which proves the null
space.  The rank deficiency follows from the rank--nullity
theorem.
\end{proof}

\noindent Proposition~\ref{prop:hover} states that the parameters
$m$ and $d_z$ are \emph{structurally} non-identifiable at level
hover.  No algorithm can resolve them from translational
measurements alone; the only way to break the ambiguity is to
introduce thrust-direction variation, that is, attitude tilt.

\subsection{Persistency of excitation under agile flight}
\begin{proposition}[Persistency of excitation]\label{prop:pe}
Let the thrust direction $\vect a(t)$ and the thrust magnitude
$f(t)$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{T}\!\int_{t-T}^{t}\!\big[f(\tau)\vect a(\tau)-\bar c\big]\!\big[f(\tau)\vect a(\tau)-\bar c\big]^{\!\top} d\tau \,\succeq\, \alpha\,\mat I_3,
\label{eq:pe-cond}
\end{equation}
for some $\bar c\in\R^3$ and some $\alpha>0$.  Then
$\mat G(t,T)\succeq \beta\,\mat I_4$ for some $\beta>0$, hence
$\vect\theta_T$ is identifiable on the window.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Condition~\eqref{eq:pe-cond} is a non-degeneracy condition on
the time-average of the rank-one outer product of the first
column of $\Phi$ relative to its mean $\bar c$.  Combined with
the identity
$\frac{1}{T}\int\mat I_3^\top\mat I_3\,d\tau=\mat I_3$ for the
remaining columns, it implies that no non-zero linear
combination of the columns of $\Phi$ averages to zero, and
consequently that $\mat G$ has empty null space.  The bound
$\beta>0$ follows from the Schur complement and the bounded
smallest singular value of $\Phi$ over the window.
\end{proof}

\noindent Geometrically, \eqref{eq:pe-cond} requires the
trajectory of $\vect a(t)$ to span the relevant directions in
$\R^3$.  Hover keeps $\vect a$ fixed at $\vect e_3$ and violates
the condition; agile flight, by contrast, induces strong
attitude tilt and thereby spans the unit sphere over the window.

\subsection{Empirical conditioning}
We compute the empirical condition number $\kappa(\mat G(t,T))$
on the actual MuJoCo flight data for three regimes: synthetic
hover, a slow Lissajous ($v_{peak}\!\approx\!3$\,m/s) and an
agile Lissajous ($v_{peak}\!\approx\!12$\,m/s), each over a
$T=1$\,s window.  Results are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:gramian}.  The hover regime is numerically
singular, matching Proposition~\ref{prop:hover}.  The slow
regime is identifiable but stiff.  The agile regime is well
conditioned and is the operating point of the MHE in the rest of
the paper.

\subsection{Decoupling of the actuator parameters}
\begin{lemma}[Structural decoupling]\label{lem:decoup}
The actuator parameters $k_\tau$ and $k_f$ are observable from
signals that are not used by the translational
regressor~\eqref{eq:regressor}.  Specifically, $k_\tau$ is
identifiable from $(\vect\omega,\vect\omega_{cmd})$ alone, and
$k_f$ is identifiable from $(T,f)$ once $f$ is reconstructed from
$(m,\dot{\vect v},\vect a)$.
\end{lemma}
\noindent This decoupling is what justifies the multi-rate
architecture proposed in Sections~\ref{sec:mhe-t}--\ref{sec:rls}:
the same data can be processed by three estimators in parallel,
each one looking at the equation in which its parameter is
identifiable.

\subsection{Implications for the architecture}
Three lessons emerge from the analysis:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Attitude is measured, not estimated.}  The thrust
direction $\vect a(t)$ enters \eqref{eq:regressor} as an
\emph{input}.  Estimating $\vect q$ as a state in the MHE adds
the nonlinearity that the Gramian analysis side-steps.
\item \emph{Excitation is regime-dependent.}  No estimator can
identify $m$ at hover; the controller must, by design, provide
enough excitation for identification to succeed.  Section
\ref{sec:results} shows that the agile Lissajous trajectory used
here is sufficient.
\item \emph{Multi-rate decoupling is natural.}
Lemma~\ref{lem:decoup} shows that the three parameters live on
three independent equations; the architecture of
Sections~\ref{sec:mhe-t}--\ref{sec:rls} simply respects this
structure.
\end{enumerate}

% ════════════════════ MHE ════════════════════
\section{Translational Moving-Horizon Estimator}\label{sec:mhe-t}

\subsection{State and dynamics}
Following Lemma~\ref{lem:idf} and Proposition~\ref{prop:pe} we
define the reduced augmented state
\begin{equation}
\vect x_T \;=\; [\,\vect p^\top,\;\vect v^\top,\;m,\;\vect d^\top\,]^\top \in \R^{10},
\end{equation}
which does \emph{not} contain the attitude quaternion or the body
rates.  The dynamics, with measured inputs $(f,\vect a)$, are
\begin{align}
\dot{\vect p} &= \vect v, \\
\dot{\vect v} &= -g\vect e_3 + (f/m)\,\vect a + \vect d, \label{eq:dv-t}\\
\dot m &= w_m, \quad \dot{\vect d} = \vect w_d, \label{eq:rw}
\end{align}
where $w_m$ and $\vect w_d$ are random-walk process-noise inputs
that allow the parameters to vary slowly with time.  The thrust
direction $\vect a$ enters \eqref{eq:dv-t} as a known time-varying
input, sourced directly from the on-board attitude estimator.

\subsection{Measurement model}
The translational MHE consumes only the translational measurements
\begin{equation}
\vect y_k \;=\; [\vect p_k,\,\vect v_k] \;\in\;\R^6.
\end{equation}
The measurement covariance is diagonal,
\[
\mat R^{-1} \;=\; \mathrm{diag}(R_p\mat I_3,\;R_{Vxy},\;R_{Vxy},\;R_{Vz}),
\]
with $R_{Vz}\gg R_{Vxy}$.  This anisotropy reflects the analysis:
the vertical residual carries most of the information about $m$
in the near-level regime, while the horizontal residuals are
contaminated by unmodelled effects (lateral drag, motor coupling)
that we prefer to absorb rather than push into the parameter.

\subsection{Arrival cost}
A standard MHE drift-prevention term penalises departures of the
initial state of the window from a prior $\bar{\vect x}_T$ with
covariance $\mat P$:
\begin{equation}
J_{\text{prior}}(\vect x_{T,0}) \;=\; \|\vect x_{T,0}-\bar{\vect x}_T\|_{\mat P^{-1}}^2.
\end{equation}
The prior is propagated each step by the one-step covariance
update of an extended Kalman filter.  We enforce
$\mat P\succeq 10^{-3}\mat I$ on the parameter block to prevent
the prior from becoming numerically singular, which empirically
removes a category of QP failures we encountered with default
acados settings.

\subsection{OCP formulation}
The MHE problem solved at each step is
\begin{align}
\min_{\vect x_T(\cdot),\,\vect w(\cdot)}\quad
& \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\!\Big[\|\vect y_k-\vect h(\vect x_{T,k})\|_{\mat R}^2 + \|\vect w_k\|_{\mat Q_w}^2\Big] \nonumber\\
& {} + \|\vect y_N-\vect h(\vect x_{T,N})\|_{\mat R}^2 + J_{\text{prior}}(\vect x_{T,0}) \nonumber\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \eqref{eq:dv-t},\;\eqref{eq:rw},\quad
m\in[m_{\min},m_{\max}],\;\vect d\in\Omega_d,
\label{eq:mhe-ocp}
\end{align}
where $\vect h(\vect x_T)=[\vect p,\vect v]$.  The OCP is
discretised with $N=31$ shooting nodes at $\dt=0.032$\,s, giving
a horizon $T\!\approx\!1$\,s consistent with the window length
used in the identifiability analysis.

\subsection{Numerical solver}
We solve \eqref{eq:mhe-ocp} with acados~\cite{verschueren2022acados}
using SQP\_RTI (a single SQP iteration per control step) and
PROJECT\_REDUC\_HESS regularisation.  The integrator is explicit
fourth-order Runge--Kutta.  With the $10$-D state and the
removed quaternion nonlinearity, a single SQP iteration completes
in $\sim$\,1.7\,ms on a desktop-class CPU; see
Section~\ref{sec:budget} for the detailed budget.

\subsection{Why the reduction matters numerically}
The conventional full-state MHE~\cite{liu2023nonlinear,kabzan2019learning}
augments $(\vect p,\vect v,\vect q,\vect\omega)$ with $(m,\vect d)$
or similar parameters.  Two specific numerical issues plague
this formulation:
\begin{itemize}
\item The Lie-invariant arrival cost
$\|\!\Logm(\vect q_{\text{meas}}^{-1}\otimes \vect q)\!\|$ has a
Jacobian that is singular at the identity, and the standard
implementation~\cite{forster2015imu} requires a hand-coded
$\sqrt{\cdot+\epsilon}$ inside the log map to avoid NaN
gradients;
\item The quaternion integration over a $1$\,s window drifts due
to accumulated linearisation error and forces frequent QP
re-projections.
\end{itemize}
By removing $\vect q,\vect\omega$ from the state we eliminate
both issues structurally.  On real-time MuJoCo data, the QP
success rate of the $10$-D MHE is $100\%$ over $63$\,s of
flight; the corresponding full-state MHE drops below $1\%$.

% ════════════════════ RLS ════════════════════
\section{RLS Identification of Actuator Time Constants}\label{sec:rls}

\subsection{Rate-loop time constant}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dw} is linear in $k_\tau=1/\tau_{rc}$.  Writing
the backward-difference approximation
\[
\dot{\vect\omega}(t)\approx \frac{\vect\omega(t)-\vect\omega(t-\dt)}{\dt},
\]
and stacking the three components, we obtain at each control
step the scalar regression
\begin{equation}
\dot{\vect\omega}(t) \;=\; k_\tau\,\big(\vect\omega_{cmd}(t-\dt)-\vect\omega(t-\dt)\big).
\label{eq:rls-ktau-step}
\end{equation}
We solve \eqref{eq:rls-ktau-step} recursively with a
forgetting-factor RLS,
\begin{align}
S_\tau(t) &= \lambda S_\tau(t-\dt) + \sum_{i=1}^3 \dot\omega_i(t)\,u_i(t-\dt), \\
N_\tau(t) &= \lambda N_\tau(t-\dt) + \sum_{i=1}^3 u_i(t-\dt)^2, \\
\hat k_\tau(t) &= \mathrm{sat}_{[1,200]}\big( S_\tau(t)\,/\,N_\tau(t) \big),
\end{align}
where $u_i = \omega_{cmd,i}-\omega_i$ and the forgetting factor
is $\lambda=0.997$, corresponding to an effective memory of
$1/(1-\lambda)\approx 333$ samples ($3.3$\,s at $100$\,Hz).  The
projection $\mathrm{sat}_{[1,200]}$ enforces physical bounds and
prevents the estimate from drifting under brief stalls.  No
horizon is required because~\eqref{eq:rls-ktau-step} is
\emph{locally} observable as soon as
$\vect\omega_{cmd}\neq\vect\omega$.

\subsection{Thrust time constant}
The thrust state $f(t)$ is not measured directly.  Once
$\hat m(t)$ is available from the translational MHE, we
reconstruct it from the translational acceleration as
\begin{equation}
\hat f(t) \;=\; \hat m(t)\,\big\|\dot{\vect v}(t)+g\vect e_3\big\|,
\label{eq:f-recon}
\end{equation}
and then identify $k_f$ from the linear lag equation
\begin{equation}
\dot{\hat f}(t) \;=\; k_f\,(T(t)-\hat f(t))
\label{eq:rls-kf-step}
\end{equation}
with the same RLS structure as for $k_\tau$,
\begin{align}
S_f(t) &= \lambda S_f(t-\dt) + \dot{\hat f}(t)\,(T(t)-\hat f(t)), \\
N_f(t) &= \lambda N_f(t-\dt) + (T(t)-\hat f(t))^2, \\
\hat k_f(t) &= \mathrm{sat}_{[10,500]}\big( S_f(t)\,/\,N_f(t) \big).
\end{align}
The reconstruction~\eqref{eq:f-recon} requires only $\hat m$ and
the translational acceleration, so the data path remains
decoupled from the rotational equation.

\subsection{Multi-rate scheduling}
The three estimators run at their natural rates:
\begin{itemize}
\item Translational MHE at $33$\,Hz (every third $100$\,Hz step);
\item RLS-rate at $100$\,Hz;
\item RLS-thrust at $100$\,Hz.
\end{itemize}
The RLS estimators are non-blocking and add a fixed
$\sim$\,5\,$\mu$s per call; the MHE consumes most of the
parameter-estimation budget.  Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline}
summarises the per-step pipeline.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Multi-rate parameter estimation per $100$\,Hz step.}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Inputs:} $(\vect p,\vect v,\vect q,\vect\omega), (T,\vect\omega_{cmd})$ at time $t$.
\STATE \textbf{Compute thrust direction:} $\vect a \leftarrow \vect R(\vect q)\vect e_3$.
\STATE \textbf{RLS-rate update:} \\
\quad $u \leftarrow \vect\omega_{cmd}-\vect\omega$;
$\dot\omega \leftarrow (\vect\omega(t)-\vect\omega(t-\dt))/\dt$. \\
\quad Update $(S_\tau,N_\tau)$ with $\lambda$;
$\hat k_\tau \leftarrow \mathrm{sat}(S_\tau/N_\tau)$.
\STATE \textbf{RLS-thrust update:} \\
\quad $\hat f \leftarrow \hat m\|\dot{\vect v}+g\vect e_3\|$;
$\dot{\hat f}\leftarrow (\hat f(t)-\hat f(t-\dt))/\dt$. \\
\quad Update $(S_f,N_f)$;
$\hat k_f \leftarrow \mathrm{sat}(S_f/N_f)$.
\IF{$t$ is a multiple of $3\dt$ \;(i.e., $33$\,Hz)}
   \STATE \textbf{MHE update:} \\
   \quad Slide window; set inputs $(f,\vect a)$ from buffer. \\
   \quad Solve \eqref{eq:mhe-ocp} with acados SQP\_RTI. \\
   \quad Read $\hat m,\hat{\vect d}$ from the latest stage.
\ENDIF
\STATE Apply EMA smoothing
$\hat m \leftarrow \alpha_m \hat m_{\text{prev}} + (1-\alpha_m)\hat m_{\text{MHE}}$
(idem for $\hat{\vect d}$).
\STATE \textbf{Return:} $(\hat m, \hat{\vect d}, \hat k_\tau, \hat k_f)$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Smoothing}
Outputs of all three estimators are passed through an
exponential moving-average (EMA) low-pass with time constants
$\sim$\,0.5\,s for the parameters $(m,\tau_{rc},\tau_f)$ and
$\sim$\,0.12\,s for the disturbance $\vect d$.  The EMA absorbs
step changes introduced by window slide and removes
high-frequency oscillations without introducing bias.

% ════════════════════ SETUP ════════════════════
\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Simulator}
We use MuJoCo~\cite{todorov2012mujoco} as the high-fidelity
simulator.  The vehicle is the \texttt{quadrotor\_acro\_motors}
model, total mass $m^{\text{true}}=1.05$\,kg, body inertia
matrix taken from the model file, four rotors with motor and
propeller dynamics, and a body-rate-controlled inner loop.
Sensor noise is added externally to position
($\sigma_p=0.005$\,m), velocity ($\sigma_v=0.02$\,m/s), and
angular rate ($\sigma_\omega=0.01$\,rad/s).  The thrust direction
$\vect a$ is sourced from the MuJoCo attitude with
$\sigma_q=0.005$\,rad per axis.

\subsection{Controller}
The outer-loop controller is a nonlinear MPC solved with
acados~\cite{verschueren2022acados}, with $N=31$ shooting nodes,
prediction horizon $T_h=1.5$\,s, and state
$(\vect p,\vect v,\vect q,\vect\omega)$.  The controller runs at
$100$\,Hz.  The reference is a temporal Lissajous of the form
$\vect r(t)=[A_x\sin(\omega_x t),\,A_y\sin(\omega_y t+\phi),\,z_0]$
with $A_x=A_y=2.5$\,m, $z_0=1.5$\,m, and amplitude swept to reach
the target peak speed.  We report a representative agile case
at peak speed $12$\,m/s.

\subsection{Parameter initialisation}
The estimator is initialised deliberately far from the true
values:
\[
m_0 = 0.90\;\text{kg},\quad
\tau_{rc}^0 = 0.030\;\text{s},\quad
\tau_f^0 = 0.10\;\text{s}.
\]
The true values, recovered from offline batch identification,
are $m=1.05$\,kg, $\tau_{rc}\approx 0.056$\,s,
$\tau_f\approx 0.009$\,s.  The initial bias is therefore $-14\%$
for $m$, $-46\%$ for $\tau_{rc}$ and $+1011\%$ for $\tau_f$.

\subsection{Metrics}
We report:
\begin{itemize}
\item Real-time parameter estimates over a $63$\,s run, with the
first half discarded as the convergence transient;
\item Tracking RMSE of the NMPC closed loop with and without
identified parameters;
\item Per-step computational cost of each estimator;
\item QP success rate of the MHE versus the full-state MHE
baseline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Velocity sweep: identification quality vs excitation}
We run the full multi-rate pipeline at six trajectory speeds by
scaling the Lissajous angular frequency
$w\!\in\!\{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5,3.0\}$\,rad/s, which yields
measured peak speeds from $\sim$\,3 to $\sim$\,12\,m/s.  All six
runs start from the same wrong nominals
$(m_0,\tau_{rc}^0,\tau_f^0)\!=\!(0.90,0.030,0.10)$.
Fig.~\ref{fig:vsweep} plots the converged estimates as a function
of the per-run $v_{peak}$, and Table~\ref{tab:converged} reports
the numerical values.

The pattern is monotone and saturates above
$v_{peak}\!\approx\!10$\,m/s, exactly as predicted by the
identifiability analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\hat m$ is recovered to within $\pm 6\%$ across the entire
range; the translational acceleration excites the $f\vect a$
column of $\Phi$ (eq.~\eqref{eq:regressor}) even at modest
speed.
\item $\hat\tau_{rc}$ improves from $0.145$\,s at
$v\!\approx\!3$\,m/s (a factor $\sim$\,3 off truth) to
$0.058$\,s at $v\!\approx\!12$\,m/s (within $4\%$ of truth).
Slow trajectories generate gentle rate commands and the
RLS-rate residual $\vect\omega_{cmd}-\vect\omega$ is dominated
by measurement noise.
\item $\hat\tau_f$ follows the same pattern, from $34$\,ms at
the slow end to $8\!-\!9$\,ms at the agile end, with the same
mechanism: low excitation $\Rightarrow$ low signal-to-noise in
the thrust-loop residual.
\end{itemize}
At hover, both actuator estimators would receive essentially
zero excitation and the translational MHE would be singular by
Proposition~\ref{prop:hover}.  The sweep thus interpolates the
two endpoints of the analysis empirically: $v_{peak}\!\to\!0$ is
non-identifiable, and $v_{peak}\!\gtrsim\!10$ yields all three
parameters within a few percent of their true values.  The
rate-loop nominal of $0.030$\,s assumed by the standard NMPC is
therefore corrected by almost a factor of two once the agile
regime is reached.

\subsection{Online versus offline reconciliation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:off} contrasts the full-model offline MHE
(replayed on the same flight log) against the often-used
\emph{$z$-shortcut} estimator
\begin{equation}
\hat m_z(t) \;=\; \frac{T(t)\,(\vect R\vect e_3)_z(t)}{\dot v_z(t)+g}.
\label{eq:z-shortcut}
\end{equation}
The shortcut returns $\hat m_z\approx 1.02$\,kg, a $-3\%$ bias
relative to the true value, but \emph{assumes} a perfect
translational model.  The full-model MHE returns
$\hat m\approx 1.11$\,kg, a $+6\%$ bias, with the honest
residual attributable to unmodelled horizontal effects.  The
true mass $1.05$\,kg lies between the two methods.

\centering

(rigorous, $1.11$\,kg) versus $z$-shortcut (perfect-model

the two methods; the full-model MHE is the only one whose

\label{fig:off}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Regime study: NMPC closed-loop benefit}
We test two deployment strategies of the same NMPC controller on
the same trajectory:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{A:}] NMPC configured with the \emph{nominal/wrong}
parameters $(m=0.90,\tau_{rc}=0.030)$;
\item[\textbf{B:}] NMPC configured with the \emph{identified}
parameters $(m=1.05,\tau_{rc}=0.056)$.
\end{itemize}
Tracking RMSE on the agile run is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:nmpc}.  The two configurations are
indistinguishable: the relative difference of $-2.6\%$
(configuration B nominally worse) is comparable to typical
run-to-run variation.  The same test repeated with online
injection (identified parameters updated every $100$\,Hz step)
yields similar magnitudes.

The empirical conclusion is unambiguous: in this fast-feedback
regime, correcting the model parameters does not measurably
alter the closed-loop tracking.  The $100$\,Hz feedback absorbs
the model mismatch within its own bandwidth, and the residual
error is dominated by trajectory dynamics rather than model
bias.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the regimes in
which the identification would matter.

\subsection{Computational budget}\label{sec:budget}
On an Intel i7-class CPU running Ubuntu 22.04, the translational
MHE (acados SQP\_RTI on $10$ states, $N=31$ shooting nodes)
solves in $\sim$\,1.7\,ms per step, the two RLS updates are
negligible (a handful of scalar operations each), and the NMPC
itself runs in $\sim$\,2\,ms.  The full parameter-estimation
pipeline thus consumes well under $4$\,ms per step, leaving
ample headroom inside the $10$\,ms control period.

\subsection{Comparison with a monolithic full-state MHE}
For completeness we re-implemented a $21$-D full-state MHE
following~\cite{liu2023nonlinear} on the same flight data, with
the Lie-invariant arrival cost and the
$\sqrt{\|\vect q_v\|^2+\epsilon}$ numerical fix for the log-map
Jacobian~\cite{forster2015imu}.  The full-state QP failed on
$99.2\%$ of the steps over $63$\,s, returning
\texttt{ACADOS\_MINSTEP}.  The corresponding mass estimate, when
a fallback rule re-injected the previous mass on failure,
slowly drifted to a meaningless saturated value.  By contrast,
the $10$-D reduced MHE proposed here succeeds on $100\%$ of the
steps on the same data.  This is, to our knowledge, the first
quantitative documentation of this failure mode and its
state-space remedy.

% ════════════════════ DISCUSSION ════════════════════
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the $5\%$ mass residual is not aerodynamic drag}
Quadratic drag, $-c_{drag}\vect v\|\vect v\|$, added to
\eqref{eq:dv-t} moves $\hat m$ in the wrong direction (toward
$0.95$\,kg) and inflates its dispersion; a negative $c_{drag}$
would be required to absorb the bias, which is unphysical.  The
residual is therefore not predominantly aerodynamic.  We
attribute it to (i)~thrust-mapping nonlinearity during fast
transients, (ii)~node-level discretisation of a jerky thrust
command, and (iii)~unmodelled rotor wake / motor coupling
effects that scale sub-linearly with~$\|\vect v\|$.  Modelling
these explicitly would require a higher-fidelity dynamic model
and is left to future work.

\subsection{Why $\tau_f$ is sub-loop, and when to compensate}
With $\hat\tau_f\approx 9$\,ms and a $100$\,Hz control loop
($\dt=10$\,ms), the thrust actuator settles in one sample and is
invisible to the controller.  We tested a feed-forward
compensation
\[
T_{applied} \;=\; T_{des} + \tau_f\,\dot T_{des}
\]
on the closed-loop NMPC and observed a $-3.5\%$ tracking-RMSE
change (i.e., slightly worse), because the differentiation
amplifies the jerky thrust command.  The compensation is only
beneficial when the control bandwidth approaches $1/\tau_f$,
which would happen at $\sim$\,200--500\,Hz.  Identifying $\tau_f$
thus serves a \emph{bandwidth-planning} purpose: it provides the
maximum useful control rate, even if the present $100$\,Hz NMPC
does not benefit.

\subsection{Connection to INDI}
The natural consumer of $\hat\tau_{rc}$ is incremental nonlinear
dynamic inversion~\cite{smeur2016adaptive,sun2022comparative},
whose stability hinges on a correct estimate of the rotational
control effectiveness.  In INDI, $\tau_{rc}$ enters the inverse
command directly, and the $-46\%$ nominal bias measured here
would translate into a stability-margin loss of comparable
magnitude.  We therefore see the RLS-rate identification
proposed here as a drop-in module for INDI controllers, where
the benefit would not be obscured by the feedback dominance of
NMPC.

\subsection{Methodological lesson: do not estimate the measured}
The single most impactful design choice in this paper is the
removal of the quaternion from the MHE state.  This is not a
clever trick but a recognition of an obvious fact: the attitude
is already measured by the IMU/odometry, the controller does not
consume the MHE's filtered version (raw odometry feeds the
NMPC), and the long-window quaternion integration is the single
most fragile element of the QP.  Estimating only the
\emph{unmeasured} parameters and feeding the measured kinematics
as inputs converts a fragile $\sim$\,21-D nonlinear MHE into a
robust $10$-D one.

\subsection{Limitations}
\paragraph*{Simulation-only validation}
The experimental validation is conducted entirely in MuJoCo
with sensor-noise injection.  The identifiability analysis, the
multi-rate architecture and the numerical remedy of the
quaternion-state fragility are platform-independent and would
transfer to hardware unchanged.  The specific bias values, on
the other hand, are MuJoCo-specific; a hardware experiment would
recover different values for $(m,\tau_{rc},\tau_f)$ but the
methodology is unaffected.  Hardware validation is left to
future work.
\paragraph*{Inertia not identified}
Body inertia $\mat J$ does not appear in our model because the
inner-loop rate controller already absorbs it.  Identifying
$\mat J$ would require opening the inner loop or adding moment
measurements and is beyond the scope of this paper.
\paragraph*{Excitation budget}
The agile Lissajous used here provides ample excitation.  In
applications where the trajectory is dictated externally
(e.g., hovering payload delivery) the controller must inject
excitation explicitly; designing such excitation signals under
safety constraints is a known open
problem~\cite{wuest2019online}.

% ════════════════════ CONCLUSION ════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper has presented an observability-aware multi-rate
estimator that recovers the quadrotor mass and the actuator
time constants $(m,\tau_{rc},\tau_f)$ on-line from in-flight
data.  A closed-form identifiability analysis predicts and
quantifies the agile-flight excitation needed for the
translational parameters to become recoverable.  A decoupled
multi-rate architecture estimates each parameter at the time
scale and on the equation where it is identifiable: a $10$-D
translational MHE at $33$\,Hz for $m$ and $\vect d$, and two
RLS estimators at $100$\,Hz for $\tau_{rc}$ and $\tau_f$.  The
resulting pipeline solves the underlying QP on $100\%$ of the
steps over $63$\,s of agile flight, with a per-step
parameter-estimation cost below $4$\,ms.

A simulation-based regime study on a MuJoCo high-fidelity
simulator quantifies the actual gap between nominal and
identified parameters: the rate-loop time constant is
corrected by almost a factor of two, the mass by $\approx 5\%$,
and the thrust constant by an order of magnitude.  In the
fast-feedback regime of a $100$\,Hz NMPC the identification's
value lies in model fidelity (commissioning, digital-twin
calibration) rather than in direct tracking gain; in
INDI-style and faster-bandwidth controllers, where the
parameters enter the control law directly, the benefit would
be commensurate with the bias corrected.

Future work includes hardware validation on a physical
platform and integration of $\hat\tau_{rc}$ into an INDI rate
controller.
